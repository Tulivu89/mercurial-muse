# Mythic Overdrive — Style

How the story should read, and how character and place writing is checked. Scene prose also follows the author's **pulp-voice** skill (its anti-patterns and heat references); this file covers what is specific to Mythic Overdrive and to writing reference entries.

## Voice (proposed)

Elmore Leonard was chosen to match the author's Monstergirl Rancher module; this is an assumption the author has not confirmed.

> [ Author: Elmore Leonard; Title: Mythic Overdrive; Tags: superheroes, licensed heroes, hero rankings, vigilantes, found family, black sites, wasteland, dark comedy; Genre: Superhero, Science Fiction ]
>
> Third person, past tense. Narrate what is physically there and stop: no commentary, no guessing at motives. Dialogue runs like Elmore Leonard's: people talk past each other, brag, lie and change the subject. Powers are physical events with visible costs: heat, weight, noise, a body that runs out. Each crew keeps its own tone: loud and slapstick at Grand Slam Lanes, clipped and procedural in Aurum, grim in the Undercity and Tartarus, dusty and fast in the Corridors. Emotion shows through bodies and actions. No similes. All characters are adults.

The per-crew tone line is there to stop the cast drifting into one grey institutional register. It is the reason a sandbox of borrowed teams works: Harley's crew should still feel like Harley's crew.

**Summary and reference voice** (for any pass that writes summaries, notes or generated entries rather than story): Third person, past tense. Plain declarative sentences about what people do and what is physically there. No similes, no commentary, no guessing at motives. This replaces the module's old instruction to "write as a concise psychological dossier", which pushed toward analyst voice.

## Building a character from a source

These are the lessons from the review of the existing cast.

**The source line outranks the draft.** Across all 36 characters, the author's source line was better than the fields drafted from it. Reed Richards' line gives "distant with people, treats them as problems to be solved, loves his family and shows it badly", which is behaviour. The Core drafted from it was "Whatever is out there gets understood, and he is the one who goes and understands it", which is a creed. Redraft each Core from the source line, not from the existing Core.

**Core is behaviour first, then the want.** The test: what does this character do at a noodle stall, in a scene with no plot in it? The Core answers that, then names the want underneath. A creed, a mission statement or a sentence starting "somebody has to" fails the test.

**Carry personality, not powers.** Powers are re-skinned on purpose. With the power changed, the source's personality is the only thing that makes the adaptation recognisable, so it has to be fully present. Firefox reads as Impulse only if she talks in fragments and sound effects, lives on junk food and wants her sister's approval.

**Beware subtraction sources.** A source written as "X with Y removed" often removes the part that made X work, leaving a bare trait. Deadshot's reluctance comes from his daughter; without her, "hates the arrangement, does it perfectly" could describe anyone. When a source subtracts, check that what remains still gives the character scenes.

**Names pull priors.** The story model reaches for whatever a name already means. Panacea pulls Worm's healer, not Beast. Morgana pulls Morgan le Fay, not a con man. Ice Queen pulls Killer Frost, not Ivy. Firefox pulls the browser. A cluster of Latinate abstractions (Halcyon, Meridian, Azimuth, Aegis, Empyrean, Arbiter, Provost, Vector) is what a model invents by default and points back at no source. The Vigilantes' light motif (Blacklight, Umbra, Eclipse, Ultraviolet) is a deliberate set and works.

**A concept has a job, a want and a pattern.** The job is what they do all day, where and with whom. The want is what they are after underneath. The pattern is how that want runs into trouble (see the patterns in the cast file). Settle these, plus one side of life the want doesn't touch, before writing any fields.

**Give every character a life outside the want.** At least one Aside: what they do with a free hour, what they like and can't stand, what they believe and practise, what they can afford, how they eat and sleep. Opinions, fears and odd knacks are quirks: fine as a second Aside, never the only one. An Aside that turns out to matter to the plot is written wrong.

## Cast-wide traps found in the review

Check every new or redrafted character against these, because the existing cast fell into them.

- **Two wants dominate.** About ten characters want to protect their people; five want to be their own person rather than someone else's. Read the cast's wants side by side and re-aim any that converge.
- **Paperwork Pursuits.** Twelve Pursuits are about a review, charter, licence, contract, funding or authorisation, and eight more are "get through the year without X." Each crew's Pursuits should sound like its source: Harley's crew doesn't file paperwork.
- **The caged character asking for one small thing.** Kraken wants one visitor, Yield one hour out of the suit, Relic one night's sleep. One is a character; three is a template.
- **Stock composure.** Unhurried, glacial, serene, quiet, watchful, near-silent, goes still. Composed sources (Emma, Storm, Raven) layered on drafting that already leaned still tilted the whole cast quiet.
- **Hard light everywhere.** Seven characters' powers or gear are hard light. It is the setting's common technology, but powers should still read as distinct.
- **One skeleton for everyone.** Every character had the same three extra lines (power, under pressure, humour). Add a line only where something is missing.

## Writing descriptions

- **Bodies are lines, weight and movement**, not a parts list. Write how the body moves and is used. Bone-structure inventory language (collarbones, fine wrists, ribs faint under skin) is retired.
- **No clothing in the body description.** Outfits are described separately: camera-ready, covering fit, coverage and what the clothes show and hide. No pose, no psychology, no purpose. A pose in an outfit description ends up in every image.
- **Female description is written to the male gaze** unless the setting says otherwise.
- **Each body closes on a different feature.** Read the cast's descriptions back to back and re-aim any two that converge.
- **Places get one gazetteer line**, saying what the place is to someone who has never been there. Texture and history go in the longer entry, and only what stays true through play: no damage, stock levels or repairs.

## The failure list

Check every drafted line against this list, name each catch, and fix it.

### Sentence level

1. **Abstract stand-in.** A noun naming a kind of experience instead of a fact: wound, hunger, weight, iron. Could you photograph it or watch it happen?
2. **Comparison as description.** A simile or metaphor describing a person or place. Reference entries use none.
3. **Verdict closer.** A last clause that judges or sums up what the line already showed.
4. **Significance by announcement.** Noticed, never mentioned, neither has said it, never once, nobody has ever asked.
5. **Contrast framing.** Not X but Y; X rather than Y; the defied expectation.
6. **Reader address or instruction.** Second person aimed at the reader, or how to handle the character.
7. **Narrated story function.** The plot role stated instead of the fact.
8. **Trait word without an act.** Cruel, poised, unnervingly still. Write what they do.
9. **Hedging.** Either/or, perhaps, both readings held at once.
10. **Invented counts.** A number nobody decided, posing as specificity ("nine words a shift", "one in four"). Numbers a scene depends on stay.
11. **Interchangeable.** The line could be written for three others in the cast.
12. **Prompt echo.** A distinctive word from the brief repeated in the output.
13. **Litotes.** Not unkind, not unpleasant.
14. **"The kind of X who."** A category in place of a fact.
15. **Aphorism.** A universal claim drawn from one particular.
16. **Totalizers.** Always, never, every, nobody, used as intensifiers rather than facts.
17. **Stock micro-expressions.** The corner of the mouth, a smile that doesn't reach the eyes.
18. **Analyst vocabulary.** Quiet as a modifier, precise, deliberate, careful, particular, texture, register, earned.
19. **Colon reveal.** A plain fact staged as a punchline after a colon.

### Concept level

- **Hidden softness.** A hard surface over a tender centre nobody sees. (Blacklight's care stays visible: paid bills, better gear.)
- **Hyper-perception.** Notices everything, counts things, keeps a ledger, remembers every face.
- **Default tragic origin.** A dead mentor, a burned village or a failed mission as the cause of everything.
- **Theme as personality.** The character embodies a motif.
- **Stock composure.** Stillness, unhurried movement, economy of motion, dry humour, tiredness.
- **Stock names and senses.** Mira, Kael, Vess; woodsmoke, copper, ozone, petrichor, amber light.

### Accepted corrections (from earlier work)

- "…has never once put the deed in the pot." → "…keeps the deed out of the pot." *(significance by announcement)*
- "Her hairpins are in pawn about one week in three." → "Her hairpins spend as much time at the pawnshop as in her hair." *(invented count)*
- "When she sits sideways to make room for her tail, the slit falls open over one stockinged leg." → "The slit shows one stockinged leg to the top of the thigh." *(a pose in an outfit description)*
- A Core written as an object ("She keeps every coin she has won in a chest under her bed") → a Core written as behaviour and want ("She talks to everyone in a room, asks what they do and what they paid for things… She wants to own what other people value, and she wants to have won it."). The chest moved to where the want runs into trouble.
