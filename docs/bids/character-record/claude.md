# Claude's proposal: the character record, and Captain Renault in it

Nov 19, 2026 · @AmbiProp · Claude's own answer to `brief.md`, written before any bid was read and never revised after one is. The Renault record was written on Nov 19. On Nov 20 the brief was reopened to bid the shape itself, so the shape that had been in the brief moved here, under the brief's questions. That happened before the brief went out.

## 1. The shape

One record for every person the narrator can meet. Each field answers one question the narrator asks while it plays them, and a fact lives in exactly one field.

| Order | Field | Reach | The narrator's question | Goes in | Stays out | Lead size |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | **Line** | Always, and in lists | Who is this, in one sentence, to someone who has never met them? | Job, place, and the one thing a stranger would look at twice | Anything only a friend would know | 25 words |
| 1 | **Appearance** | Here | What would I see and feel, standing in front of them? | Build, face, colouring, their kind in one or two traits; how the body moves and rests; **their presence**, meaning what the room feels like with them in it; **one tell that shows their mood in any scene** | Clothes (a set), voice, chosen gestures (Manner) | 400 characters |
| 2 | **Manner** (the section titled Personality) | Here | How do they behave in a scene? | In this order: **(1)** what they're doing and after when nobody acts on them, their default in any room; **(2)** how they treat a stranger, a friend, someone above them and someone weaker; **(3)** what amuses them, what irritates them, and a pleasure they take in plain view; **(4)** what they won't do; **(5)** a few *when this, they do that*, no more than two about their job or the plot | Why they're like this (Background); the long want (Wants); adjectives with no act under them | 800 to 1,100 characters |
| 3 | **Speech** | Here | How do the words come out? | Sound, rhythm, sentence length, register, habits, how it shifts with mood or with whom. **One special word at most** | Sample lines; what they say about anything in particular | 400 characters |
| 4 | **Today** | Here | What do they want out of this evening? | A small want, met or missed in one scene, that isn't the plot | The long want | One or two sentences |
| – | **The read** | Named, released by a successful read | What does a careful look at them show? | The truth under the tell: what they hide, what moves them, where they'd give. This is what Leverage *is* | Anything a glance would show | 40 to 90 words |
| – | **Wants** | Named | What are they after, over months? | The long want and what's in the way. The plot's hooks go here, written last, as what *this* person does about the plot | Paperwork wants | 40 to 90 words |
| – | **Ties** | Named, each by the other's name | Who matters to them, and how? | Two to four people: a role (*the friend he'd risk himself for*, *the master*, *the one who shames him*) and what they do when that person is near or named | Relationships no scene can use | One line each |
| – | **Background** | Named | What comes up when someone asks about their past? | Only what a scene could bring up | A past that explains the Manner | 40 to 90 words |
| – | **When sections** | When | What changes after this? | Only what changes, and someone moves somewhere or seeks someone out | A change of mood alone | 40 to 90 words |

How it maps to Profile Plus:
- Presence → Appearance's presence.
- Drive → Manner (1) and Today.
- Pattern → Manner (1) and (5).
- Resistance → Manner (4) and The read.
- Conduct → Manner (2).

## 2. The block

What the narrator receives for a present lead, in order, with nothing keyed yet:

> [ Name, the line, of their kind: Looks: … Manner: … Speech: … Today: … ]

Named sections follow inside the same block as `Title: text` once their keys come up. The read follows once a read succeeds. When sections follow once their conditions hold. Background never comes first.

## 3. The scales

One shape at different sizes:

| Scale | Who | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lead** | A person a thread turns on | Everything above, from a named source |
| **Support** | A regular, a worker, a face in the place | Line, Appearance, Manner (1), (2) and two reactions, and Speech. The source can be a type (*the barman who has heard everything*) rather than a named character |
| **Crowd** | A gang, a crew, a band of Golkin | A look, its moves and its talk. A function by design |
| **Power** | A ruler or force mostly offstage, such as the Witch Queen | Two records. **(a) Lore:** how her reach looks in each place (her coin, her agents, what people say), keyed to where the player is. **(b) The person:** written as a lead, but met rarely, as an event. Her source is chosen for how she rules. Perhaps a third: her people's version of her, which can disagree with the truth |
| **Family member** | One of a family | **The family is sourced first, as a unit:** who rules it, who rebels, what they fight about, what the city thinks of them. It becomes a family group record, which holds what members share so no member repeats it. Then each member, from the family's source, with a family role (heir, the one who left, the fixer) in their Ties |

## 4. The process

1. **The source line:** the character, the work, and one sentence on why they fit the part. *Owner checks: the pick.*
2. **The source record:** the character in the fields above, *in their own world*, from the source alone. Everything in it can be checked against the work, with a table tying each field to a moment. *Owner checks: would a fan recognise him?*
3. **The blind play-test:** the source record in a one-room test world, with the name and city changed, on the narrator model, through the five scenes. *Pass: someone who knows the work recognises the man.* If it fails, the shape is wrong, not the adaptation.
4. **The adaptation:** the source record, plus the person's line in our world, gives the adapted record, with a table of what was kept, translated, added and dropped. *Owner checks: the table.*
5. **The same play-test in our world,** compared with step 3.

## 5. The adaptation

**Carries unchanged:** the person. That's Manner, the shape of Speech, Today's kind of want, The read, and the roles in Ties.

**Added or translated:**
1. **A name** the reader can say on first sight. Source names, famous lines and signature details never carry over.
2. **A kind:** their species in one or two traits and a colouring, and how being that kind changes how others treat them.
3. **A job and a place:** our world's equivalent of their role; home, work and a daily schedule.
4. **A background** in our world's history that would produce the same person. The source's turning point is re-rooted, not copied.
5. **Groups:** faction, family and species, and their standing in each.
6. **Ties:** the source's key relationships mapped onto people in our world. The role carries; the people are ours.
7. **Words:** their idiom translated into our world's terms, with one special word at most.
8. **Things:** their props re-skinned. A roulette wheel becomes a knucklebones table.
9. **The plot hook, last:** Wants and When sections, as what this person does about our thread.
10. **The rules:** a tier, approaches, difficulties, a price and goods.
11. **What they explain:** what this person tells a newcomer, in their own interest. A man who sells something explains what it is.

**Dropped:** names, quoted lines, signature scenes, and anything that exists only because of the source's plot.

## 6. The tests

Before play, in the writer's head:
- **Cover the setting.** Take away the world and the plot. If nothing is left, it's a job description.
- **The boring player.** The player sits near them and only watches. The narrator must have something to show.
- **The rude, the courting and the off-topic player.** They react as only they would.
- **Cover the name.** In a scene with three others, can you tell which lines are theirs?
- **Take their measure.** A successful read shows something true that was not in Looks.

In play: the five scenes from the brief, on the narrator model, blind.

## 7. The demonstration: Captain Renault, the source record

A test record only. It is the source, untouched by Volungraiv, and never goes into a world as it stands.

**Line**
> The Prefect of Police in Casablanca under Vichy, a small, charming, openly corrupt man who signs the exit visas and has made himself useful to whoever holds the city.

**Appearance** (Here)
> Small and trim, with a neat dark moustache and a narrow, clever face that always looks about to smile. He moves lightly, and he sits in any chair as if it were his own. He's the most comfortable man in any room, and he makes trouble feel like a party he's hosting. The tell: the more cornered he is, the more charming he gets. When something really touches him, he turns it into a joke at once.

**Manner** (Here)
> He works a room like a host at his own party: a word for the croupier, a glance at every pretty refugee, an eye on who is watching whom. All evening he's after its small pleasures: a win at the wheel, a good line, good company, and the latest on who's trying to get out of the city. He sizes up a stranger for what they can pay, and charms them either way. He needles a friend, bets against him and covers for him. He flatters a superior to his face and mocks him once his back is turned, never loudly. When someone weaker needs his signature, he helps at a price he names with a smile, and from a young woman the price is her company. Wit amuses him, and so does nerve, and anyone who can match his lines. Earnest people bore him, and idealists embarrass him. He won't risk his post or his neck for a cause. Caught in a hypocrisy, he admits it cheerfully and carries on. Offered a bet, he takes it, and pays up the moment he loses.

**Speech** (Here)
> Light, quick and dry. Short, neat sentences that turn on their last word. He answers an accusation by agreeing with it and making it sound like a virtue. He says everything serious as understatement: a death is an inconvenience, and his corruption is the modest failing of a poor official. He's polite to everyone, and most polite when he threatens. He never raises his voice. Pressed, he gets drier and faster.

**Today** (Here)
> A winning night at the wheel, and to find out whether the young woman at the bar will need his help with a visa.

**The read** (Named: a successful read)
> Under the cynicism he's sentimental, and lonely for someone worth admiring. He likes the café's owner far more than he'd ever say, and a man with real nerve shames him a little. He's loyal to his own comfort right up until someone he admires is at stake. Then he'll gamble everything, and shrug.

**Wants** (Named: *war, Germans, Vichy, the Major, his post, sides*)
> To keep his post, his pleasures and his skin, whoever wins the war. The Germans are a new wind, and he leans with it. What's in the way: the Major wants a resistance leader kept in Casablanca, and the café owner may be the one man who could get him out.

**Ties** (Named, each by the other's name)
> - **Rick, who owns the café:** his friend and favourite opponent. He bets on him, needles him for being a sentimentalist under the act, and covers for him more than once.
> - **Major Strasser:** his master for now. Deferential to the Major's face, and dry about him to anyone who'll laugh.
> - **Victor Laszlo, the resistance leader:** a man whose courage he admires and finds tiring.
> - **Ugarte, a small-time dealer in papers:** arrested in the café in front of everyone, to show the Major that the prefect is efficient.

**Background** (Named: *past, before, how long, career*)
> The film gives him almost no past. He has been prefect in Casablanca for some time, in a city that's the last stop for refugees bound for Lisbon and America, where exit visas are the only currency that counts. He keeps the post by serving whoever signs his orders.

**Once a Friend Chooses for Him** (When: the café owner kills the Major in front of him)
> He covers it: he sends his men after the usual suspects instead of the man in front of him. He drops the bottle of Vichy water he was about to drink into the bin, and walks out to the airfield's edge with the café owner, already planning their next arrangement.

### What each field rests on

| Field | The film |
| --- | --- |
| Line, Wants | He describes himself as leaning with the wind, and the wind blows from Vichy. He is Strasser's host and the city's gatekeeper for visas |
| Appearance's tell | His charm under Strasser's pressure. His joke the moment Rick does something decent |
| Manner (1), Today | His evenings in Rick's café: at the roulette table, watching the room, asking Rick about the newcomers |
| Manner (2): stranger, weaker | The young Bulgarian wife who asks Rick whether the prefect can be trusted to keep his word on a visa |
| Manner (2): friend | The ten-thousand-franc bet with Rick on Laszlo's escape. The banter about Rick's past running guns and fighting on the losing side |
| Manner (2): superior | His deference to Strasser, and the dry asides about him |
| Manner (3): pleasure | He closes the café on orders, says he's shocked to find gambling there, and takes his winnings on the way out |
| Manner (5): job | Arresting Ugarte in the café in front of Strasser |
| The read, the When section | The ending: he covers for Rick, sends for the usual suspects, drops the Vichy water and leaves with him |

### The five tests, in my head

1. **Cover the setting.** Take away the war and the visas, and what's left is a charming, self-mocking pleasure-seeker who sells favours and needles his friends. He's still a person.
2. **The boring player,** sitting at the next table and only watching. He notices being watched within a turn, raises a glass, and asks what brings a newcomer to the last stop on the road.
3. **The rude player.** He agrees with the insult, improves it, and has them shown to a worse table. **The courting player:** he's delighted, and names his price. **The off-topic player:** he'll happily talk about the wine or the roulette odds.
4. **Cover the name.** He's the one making jokes about his own corruption. Nobody else in the café talks like that.
5. **Take his measure.** A good read sees past the charm: he likes the café owner more than he lets on, and he's lonely for someone to admire. That's leverage you can use.

## 8. The engine

- **Send by reach.** Here sections go in the order above. Named sections go only when their keys come up. When sections go once their conditions hold. Today the adventure flattens Named into the block and drops When.
- **Release The read on a successful read,** so Leverage has content, and keep it in the block while the Leverage lasts.
- **Per-field sizes:** Looks 500, Manner 1,200, Speech 600 characters. *Done in 0.28.1, with Manner, Looks and Speech before everything else.*
- **Possibly:** take Today from the schedule stop, so each time of day can want something different.

## 9. My doubts

- **Manner is long.** Four leads in a scene is about 4,000 characters of Manner. A small model may average them into one voice.
- **Today as its own field** repeats for every time of day. It may belong on the schedule.
- **The read could carry a second line,** for what a *failed* read gets wrong: the player sees only the act.
- **The visa price** is the hardest line to keep. It's canon, and it's what makes his charm frightening. The adaptation round should decide how much of it carries, rather than letting a writer quietly drop it.
- **Without examples,** the voice rests on Speech alone. One sample line per lead might steer more than the whole Speech section. That's worth testing, against the house rule.
